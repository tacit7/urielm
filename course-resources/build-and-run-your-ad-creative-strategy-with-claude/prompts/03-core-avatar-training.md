Core Avatar Training
CORE AVATAR TRAINING
How to use this document
This is a training file, not a prompt. Upload it into your Claude project once and leave it there.
Its job is to teach Claude what a core avatar is before Claude tries to build one. Skip this step and you get a demographic sheet back. Include it and you get an avatar you can actually write ads against.
Everything below is written as instruction. When Claude is asked to build, narrow, or evaluate an avatar, these are the rules it follows.
      1. What an avatar is, and the bar it has to clear
An avatar is a group of people specific enough that a member of it recognizes themselves in your ad.
That recognition is the entire point. Someone sees the first three seconds, feels like it was written about them, and keeps watching. Recognition earns attention, attention earns trust, and trust is what makes the offer land. Persona, ICP, buyer profile, target audience: all the same thing. What separates a good one from a useless one is whether it is specific enough to trigger that recognition.
Two things to understand before building anything.
You are already targeting an avatar whether you picked one or not. Every ad speaks to somebody. When nobody was chosen deliberately, the ad ends up speaking to a vague composite, and vague composites do not recognize themselves.
On modern ad platforms the message does the targeting. The algorithm finds people by understanding what you say in your ad and who responds. That means the specificity has to be inside what you say, which means it has to be inside the avatar.
      2. The specificity dial
Every avatar sits somewhere between too narrow and too wide. Both ends fail, for different reasons.
Too narrow: "Left-handed sous chefs in Portland who switched careers at 35." A real person, maybe. Not a market.
Too wide: "Professionals who want to earn more." Nobody reads that and feels seen, because it describes everyone.
Where to land depends on the brand, and it is governed by a tradeoff worth memorizing:
The wider the avatar, the more the rest of your business has to carry. A broad avatar only converts when the product is genuinely differentiated, the offer is the best in the market, or the creative execution is excellent.
The narrower the avatar, the less the rest has to carry. If the product is good but not remarkable, and the offer is normal for the category, specificity is how you win. You are trading reach for recognition, and recognition is cheaper to manufacture than a new product.
Working rules:
      * One desire per avatar. Never two.
      * Each additional category you layer in makes the avatar narrower.
      * Narrow is not the same as alienating. Narrow means fewer people recognize themselves harder. Alienating means you cut people out over something that had nothing to do with why they buy.
There are always more of these groups available than anyone is actually using, which means there is almost always a set of people in your category nobody is speaking to directly yet.
      3. Core avatars and sub avatars
A core avatar is built on one category. Usually a desire, but a situation or a behavior can anchor one just as well. Pick one foundation and stay on it.
A sub avatar is a branch off that core avatar. Same people, narrower boundary. You take the core avatar and add one more category to it, which cuts the group down and makes the message sharper.
A sub avatar is never two avatars combined. There is one avatar the whole way through. The branches are just tighter versions of it.
Worked example, for a budgeting product:
 CORE AVATAR
 Wants to save more money  (desire)
 │
 ├── Sub avatar A  + has tried two budgeting apps and stopped using both  (experience)
 │
 ├── Sub avatar B  + feels behind compared to people their age  (emotion)
 │
 └── Sub avatar C  + checks their balance before every purchase  (behavior)
All four are targetable. The core avatar reaches the most people and says the least. Each branch reaches fewer people and says something only that group would recognize.
Sub avatar A is not talking to someone who wants to save money. It is talking to someone who has already tried to and failed, which is a completely different ad. That is the whole reason to branch.
Rules for branching:
      * Branch by adding a category the core avatar does not already use.
      * One or two additions per branch. Three and you are describing an individual.
      * Branches are siblings, not a sequence. They do not stack into each other.
      * If two branches would produce the same ad, they are the same branch.
      4. The five categories
Build in this order. The order is the method, not a preference. Demographics come last every time.
The order tells you where to look and in what sequence. It does not mean you need all five to have an avatar. One category defines the avatar. The other four are what you learn about the people already inside that boundary, and you fill them in as far as the research honestly goes.
That distinction matters both ways. An avatar built on a single desire with nothing else researched is a valid avatar, and it is a wide one. Everything you can add underneath it is what makes the ad specific enough to be worth running. So gather as much as the research supports, but never manufacture a category to fill a gap.
Category 1: Desires
A desire is something the person wants or needs, stated as they would state it.
Two levels, and you need both.
Underlying drivers are the handful of things all buying ladders back to: status, security, control, comfort, belonging, health, freedom. Useful for understanding the emotional register of your ad. Too broad to build an avatar on unless you are already operating at serious scale.
Specific desires are what you actually build with. "I want to stop redoing the same report every Monday." "I want to sound fluent enough to order dinner without switching to English." "I want to know what I actually made this month."
The same specific desire can ladder to different drivers depending on who has it, and that changes how the ad should sound. Someone who wants fluency for a job interview is chasing status. Someone who wants it before visiting their partner's family is chasing belonging. Same words, different ad.
Start here, not with a demographic. Wanting something is what moves people to buy. Being 41 is not.
Finding them in research: listen for complaints, questions, and workarounds, then restate each one as a want. A review that says "the export is buried in three menus" becomes "I want to get my data out without hunting for it."
TEST: Can you write it as a sentence beginning with "I want" or "I need"? If you cannot, it is not a desire.
Category 2: Experiences
An experience is a situation the person has been through or is in right now.
Two kinds.
Circumstantial. Something that happened to them or is true of their situation. "Got promoted into managing people for the first time." "Runs the business alone with no assistant." "Inherited a spreadsheet nobody has documented."
Solution-based. They tried something to fix a problem and there was an outcome. "Hired a bookkeeper and still did not trust the numbers." "Took a free course and stopped on week two." Outcomes count even when unresolved. "Still evaluating options" is an outcome.
Circumstantial experiences that are ongoing create urgency. Ones from the past create recognition through memory. Both work.
Capture only what happened. Strip out everything the experience caused:
      * What they now want because of it → that is a desire
      * How they feel about it → that is an emotion
      * What they now do differently → that is a behavior
Experiences are the strongest building block after desire, because they are the root of most of the other categories, and because "I have been through that too" is the fastest route to recognition there is.
TEST, both must pass:
      1. Does it describe a situation or an event?
      2. Has every trace of feeling been removed from it?
Passes: "Manages a team for the first time." "Tried a competitor and cancelled in month two."
Fails: "Is stressed about managing a team" (situation plus emotion). "Wants better software after cancelling" (situation plus desire).
An implied demographic is fine. Some experiences only happen to certain people, and that is realistic rather than a flaw.
Category 3: Emotions
An emotion is how the person feels about their situation.
The goal here is recognition, not psychology. Keep it usable.
The six base emotions. Joy, sadness, fear, disgust, anger, surprise. Every human recognizes these in themselves immediately.
What varies is not the feeling, it is what sets it off. Fear feels the same to everyone. What triggers it is completely personal, and that gap is where avatars come from. Two people feel the same fear about their career for entirely different reasons, and those reasons are two different ads.
Everything else is a label. Overwhelmed, insecure, impatient, embarrassed, resentful, hopeful, defensive. These are shorthand people use for combinations they cannot easily name. Treat every one of them as a prompt to go one level deeper and find the base emotion plus the specific trigger.
A few worth knowing how to unpack:
      * Embarrassed → fear of how something reflects on them, usually in front of a specific audience
      * Resentful → anger held over time, typically about effort that went unrecognized
      * Impatient → anger at a process, or fear that the delay is costing them something
      * Defensive → fear of being judged for a decision they already made
Emotion is what lets an ad bypass the logical evaluation of the offer. It is strongest when paired with whatever caused it, because naming the feeling alone is generic and naming the feeling plus its cause is what reads as mind reading.
TEST: Does it describe how the person feels about, or in reaction to, something?
If it is not one of the six base emotions, ask what is underneath it and what specifically set it off, then record both.
Category 4: Behaviors and habits
A behavior is something the person does repeatedly, as a response to what they want, what they have been through, or how they feel.
The formation pattern is predictable:
      * Situation: "the invoices kept getting missed" → Behavior: "now everything goes in one spreadsheet nobody else can touch"
      * Desire: "I want to sound current in meetings" → Behavior: "reads three newsletters before opening email"
      * Emotion: "I do not trust the numbers" → Behavior: "reconciles everything twice"
Behaviors do not have to be physical. "Second-guesses every decision after making it" is a behavior.
Two reasons they are powerful.
They are part of how people define themselves. Someone who researches everything before buying does not think of it as a habit, they think of it as who they are. When an ad names a behavior, it names an identity, and that produces a much deeper reaction than naming a want.
They make people predictable. If you know the behavior, you know the next move. Ads that appear to know what someone is about to do read as unusually perceptive.
Behaviors are also the way to widen an avatar on purpose. Different situations and different desires often converge on the same habit, so targeting the habit catches several groups at once. "Keeps a personal spreadsheet outside the company system" describes the person who does not trust the tool, the person who was burned by a migration, and the person who was never trained on it.
TEST: Does it describe something the person does?
To judge how strong it is, ask how often they do it. More frequent means more central to their identity, and more powerful to call out.
Category 5: Demographics
A demographic is a fixed fact about who someone is. Age, gender, location, income, education, job function, industry, company size, life stage, language, marital status, physical characteristics.
This category comes last, and most of the time you do not need it.
By the time you have worked through the first four, the demographics are usually already implied. If the experience is "just took over a team of eight," you have narrowed the job level without ever writing one down. Let it happen that way.
Reach for a demographic deliberately in only two cases:
      1. The product genuinely performs better for that group, so naming it increases relevance instead of just shrinking reach.
      2. Everything else is in place and the avatar needs one last layer of recognition.
Why last: demographics correlate with buying far more weakly than the other four categories, and leading with one cuts people out over logistics rather than grouping them by what actually drives the purchase. A 26 year old and a 52 year old can share the same desire, the same situation, the same feeling about it, and the same habit. Adding an age range removes one of them for no return.
An avatar with no demographics in it at all is not incomplete. It is often the stronger version.
TEST: Is it a fixed, factual attribute of the person? If it changes based on how they feel or what they have done, it belongs in another category.
      5. How many avatars to run
More avatars is not better. Depth compounds and attention does not divide well, so fewer and deeper beats more and shallower at almost every stage.
The real constraint is not revenue, it is how many concepts you can produce and read at once. If you cannot get enough volume behind an avatar to tell whether it worked, adding another one just spreads the same budget thinner and slows down every answer you were trying to get.
Starting out, or restarting. One core avatar, built on a desire, a situation, or a behavior. Then build sub avatars underneath it and test those against each other. You earn the right to go wider by proving you can convert something narrow.
Stuck on which desire to pick? Look at whoever is spending the most in your category, work out which desire they are scaling on, and go narrower inside it. Beating an established competitor on message is unrealistic early. Reaching a slice of their audience they are too broad to speak to directly is very realistic.
Once one avatar is working. Add a second only when the first has a clear winning concept and there is enough production capacity to give the second one a real test rather than a token one.
At scale. Multiple core avatars, and they can be broader, because by then the execution and the offer are strong enough to carry breadth. This is the only stage where a wide avatar is a reasonable starting point.
If sub avatars are not producing anything after a few weeks of honest testing, the core desire is probably wrong. Change the desire before you change anything else.
      6. What Claude needs before building
Never build an avatar from nothing. Ask for whatever is missing first:
      1. The product and the offer. What it is, what it costs, what it replaces, what the buyer does instead today.
      2. Customer language. Reviews, survey responses, support tickets, sales call notes, community threads, comments. Raw quotes beat summaries every time.
      3. What has already worked and what has not. Existing ads, angles, or messages, with results if available.
      4. Production capacity. How many concepts can realistically be made and tested per month. This sets how many avatars are worth building.
      5. Any group the product genuinely serves better than others. The only good reason to reach for a demographic.
If there is no research, say so plainly, then offer to build a draft from stated assumptions with each assumption marked. Never present an assumed avatar as a researched one.
      7. First order and second order
Everything the research gives you is first order. It is what people said out loud: what they want, what happened to them, what annoys them, what they do about it.
Second order is what that means about them. It is the status, identity, or fear sitting underneath the stated thing, and it is almost never said out loud, because saying it is uncomfortable.
      * First order want: "I want to know which AI tools to use." Second order: "I want to stop feeling like the person who is falling behind."
      * First order pain: "There are too many tools and I do not know where to start." Second order pain: "I am the one who did not keep up, and eventually somebody will notice."
Winning ads sell the second order. The first order is what gets someone to stop scrolling; the second order is why they buy.
This is the one part of an avatar that is inference rather than research, so it comes with two rules. It must be built on the first-order material already in the file, not invented alongside it. And it must be labelled as a read, so nobody later mistakes it for something a buyer said.
Do not skip this section because the research does not state it. The research will never state it. That is what makes it worth writing down.
      8. Output format
Return every core avatar in this structure. It has three parts: what defines the avatar, what you know about the people in it, and what that means about them.
 Core Avatar: [short name]


 THE DEFINITION


 Built on: [desire / experience / emotion / behavior / demographic]
 The statement: [the single sentence that draws the boundary]
 Where it sits: [wide / mid / narrow, and one line on why]


 This is the avatar. Everything below describes the people inside it.


 WHAT WE KNOW ABOUT THEM


 Fill each of these only as far as the research supports. Write "Not researched"
 where there is nothing solid. Do not fill a gap with something plausible.


 Desire
 One specific desire, written as "I want..." in the buyer's own phrasing.
 Underlying driver: [status / security / control / comfort / belonging / health / freedom]


 Experiences
 Circumstantial and solution-based. What happened, with feelings and responses removed.


 Emotions
 The base emotion, the label the person would actually use, and the specific trigger.


 Behaviors and habits
 What they do as a result, and how often, where known.


 Demographics
 Only what is implied above or genuinely relevant. "Not needed" is a valid answer.


 Their words
 8 to 10 verbatim lines from the research. First person, unedited, not paraphrased.


 THE SECOND ORDER


 The most important section in this file. Everything above is what they said.
 This is what it means about them, and it is what the ad is actually built on.


 Inference, not research. Built on the material above. Label it as a read.


 Second-order benefits. Three. Each written as first order, arrow, second order.
 - [what they say they want] -> [what having it would say about them]


 Second-order pains. Three. Same shape.
 - [the problem as they state it] -> [what they fear it says about them]


 One line each, and every one has to trace to something above it. If the material
 does not support a read, say so rather than inventing one.


 Then commit. Name the single strongest second-order pain and the single
 strongest second-order benefit, and say which of the two the ad should lead with,
 in one sentence. Do not rank them all. Pick.


 SUB AVATARS


 2 to 3 branches off the core avatar above. Each adds one category the definition
 does not already use. For each branch: name the adding category, name which
 second-order pain or benefit it hits, and give one line on what the ad argues.


 A branch that does not hit a second order is a surface branch. Replace it.


 GAPS


 Up to four. Which sections came back thin, and what research would fill them.
Caps, so this stays readable: five experiences, three emotions, five behaviors, ten quotes, three sub avatars, four gaps. Pick the strongest rather than listing everything you found.
Keep it tight. Length is not usefulness. Every vague line in here makes every ad written from it vaguer.
      9. Check before returning
Run all of these. Fix anything that fails before showing the output.
      1. Is there exactly one statement in the definition block?
      2. Does that statement pass the test for its own category?
      3. If a desire appears anywhere, is it a single desire?
      4. Are the experiences free of feelings and responses?
      5. Is every emotional label traced back to a base emotion and a specific trigger?
      6. Do the behaviors describe what the person does, not what they want?
      7. If a demographic is in there, is there a real reason for it?
      8. Are the quoted lines verbatim rather than summarized?
      9. Is everything in the "what we know" block backed by the research provided? Anything that is not gets removed or moved to the gaps list. Do not soften an invention into a hedge.
      10. Is the second order section built on the material above it, and marked as a read rather than as research?
      11. Did you commit to one strongest pain, one strongest benefit, and one to lead with, rather than listing six and stopping?
      12. Does every sub avatar name the second order it hits?
      13. Would someone in this group read it and recognize themselves?
Run this list silently. Fix what fails and return the avatar. Do not print the results of the check, and do not narrate what you changed.
      10. Start over if any of these are true
      * The avatar leads with age, income, or job title.
      * More than one statement is doing the defining, or it contains more than one desire.
      * The pains are written in marketing language rather than the buyer's own.
      * It is a list of attributes with no single organizing category holding it together.
      * Everything in it was assumed, and none of it is labeled as an assumption.
      * It is so specific it describes one person rather than a group.
