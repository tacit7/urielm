# Naming Conventions and Data Analysis

Time range: 59:25-71:45

## Synopsis

This section moves from creating ad concepts into preparing those ads for useful analysis after launch. The instructor emphasizes that the data work has to begin before the ads run, because the names assigned in Meta determine whether later performance reports can be interpreted strategically. Instead of naming ads generically, each ad should be labeled with the concept name, persona, launch date, and the four core creative variables developed throughout the training: persona, angle, offer, and format. The point is not administrative neatness; it is preserving the creative context that explains what was actually being tested.

The instructor introduces a naming convention prompt that can turn an ad script and format description into a ready-to-use Meta ad name. Once the ads are launched with those labels, Claude and the Meta MCP can later inspect performance data and analyze not only which ad performed best, but which underlying variable or combination of variables appears to be driving the result. This is the major distinction the instructor keeps returning to: unlabeled reporting might show spend, clicks, or conversions for "video three," but structured naming lets the marketer see whether a specific persona, pain angle, offer type, or production format is resonating.

The data flowchart prompt is presented as the companion tool for post-launch analysis. Using fake sample data from a bookkeeping software example, the instructor shows how performance can be split by persona, then by angle, offer, and format. In that example, the winning path is solo founders, a tax-panic angle, a lead magnet offer, and a UGC format. The instructor identifies the winner by looking for the path with the strongest spend signal and the lowest cost per lead or cost per action, then explains that this tells the team where to lean in creatively until the idea starts to fatigue.

The Q&A extends the data-analysis discussion into diagnosing poor performance. The instructor recommends thinking of underperforming ads as a funnel and locating the first broken metric in sequence: CPM, thumb-stop or hook performance, playthrough, click-through rate, and landing-page conversion. A high CPM may mean the audience is expensive or that the platform is penalizing weak engagement. A poor thumb-stop rate points toward a hook problem, weak playthrough suggests the body of the ad is losing attention, weak click-through indicates the ad is not creating enough intent, and weak landing-page conversion suggests the post-click experience may be the issue.

The section closes with practical judgment about tools, models, and testing scope. The instructor says Claude is strong for copywriting and structured analysis, while ChatGPT may be better for image generation, and notes that higher-capacity models are useful when budget and usage limits allow. For test design, the instructor advises matching the number of variables to the available ad budget. Hooks are almost always worth testing because multiple hooks can be captured during the same production session at little extra cost, while broader variables such as format and angle should be prioritized when there is enough budget to test them meaningfully.

## Key Details

- Naming conventions should be created before launch so Meta performance data retains the strategic meaning of each ad concept.
- The recommended label structure includes the concept name, persona, launch date, and creative variables.
- The four variables carried through the analysis are persona, angle, offer, and format.
- Claude can use a naming convention prompt to generate consistent Meta ad names from a script and format description.
- The goal of labeling is to learn why something worked, not merely which individual ad had the best result.
- The data flowchart prompt turns labeled performance data into a visual breakdown by persona, angle, offer, and format.
- The sample bookkeeping software analysis compares solo founders and agency owners, then further splits results by angles and offers.
- The example winner is the solo-founder persona paired with a tax-panic angle, lead magnet offer, and UGC format.
- Winning combinations are identified through both spend allocation and efficiency metrics such as cost per lead or cost per action.
- Without structured names, reports may preserve numerical outcomes while losing the creative inputs behind those outcomes.
- Poor performance should be diagnosed as a funnel rather than treated as a single vague ad problem.
- The diagnostic sequence begins with CPM, then moves through thumb-stop rate, playthrough, click-through rate, and landing-page conversion rate.
- The first weak metric in the funnel points to the first area that should be fixed.
- The instructor suggests that $50 to $100 of spend may be enough to start reading early directional metrics, depending on the goal and optimization event.
- Model choice depends partly on usage limits: stronger models can be worthwhile with sufficient budget, while cheaper models may be better when usage runs out quickly.
- Hooks are efficient to test because three or four alternate openings can be recorded with the same creator session or filming setup.
- Lower-budget testing should use fewer ads and put more care into production quality, while higher-budget testing can support more variable combinations.

## Why It Matters

- Good naming conventions turn ad accounts into learning systems instead of collections of disconnected assets.
- Structured labels make creative strategy measurable by connecting performance data back to the persona, angle, offer, and format behind each ad.
- Flowchart analysis helps marketers identify repeatable patterns rather than overreacting to one winning or losing creative.
- Preserving the "why" behind performance makes it easier to decide what to scale, what to iterate, and what to stop producing.
- Funnel-based diagnosis prevents teams from blaming the wrong part of the system, such as rewriting the offer when the hook is the actual failure point.
- Budget-aware testing keeps experimentation manageable and reduces the risk of spreading spend too thin across too many creative variables.
- Testing hooks aggressively is a practical way to gather more creative signal without materially increasing production cost.
