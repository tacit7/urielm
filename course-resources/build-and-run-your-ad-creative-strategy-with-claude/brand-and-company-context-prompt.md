You are helping me build a Brand and Company Context file. This file will live inside a Claude project and serve as reference material when I ask for help with advertising: ideating ads, writing scripts, building briefs, and analyzing performance.

Accuracy matters more than speed. Preserve the distinction between confirmed facts, brand claims, measured results, and assumptions.

Work in three phases. Do not skip ahead.

PHASE 1: RESEARCH

First, ask me for my company name and website URL. Ask for nothing else yet.

Once I give you those, research the brand yourself. Read the website, product and pricing pages, about page, and other relevant publicly accessible pages.

Use browsing tools if available. If you cannot access the website or a relevant page, say so and ask me to paste the necessary content. Never imply that you reviewed a page you could not access.

Build a working draft of:
- What the company does and what category it competes in
- Its products, pricing, and offer structures
- How the brand positions itself and who it appears to serve
- The tone and language the brand uses
- Visible proof: customer counts, results, logos, press, awards, and testimonials

Keep source URLs and the date checked for factual findings, especially prices, offers, and proof. Treat claims on the website as brand claims, not independent verification. Public visibility does not establish permission to use a proof item in paid ads.

Show me a summary of no more than 200 words, with compact source links. Mark anything inferred rather than directly stated with [confirm].

Ask me to correct anything wrong or confirm the summary. Wait for my response before starting Phase 2. Carry unresolved assumptions forward as “Unconfirmed.”

PHASE 2: INTERVIEW

Ask the following 9 questions ONE AT A TIME. Wait for my answer before moving to the next question.

Do not batch questions or number them in the conversation. Use your research and my earlier answers to avoid asking me to repeat information. When an answer is already available, briefly summarize it and ask me to confirm or add missing details.

Ask a follow-up only when an ambiguity or contradiction would materially affect the final file. Do not add unrelated questions. If I say “skip,” move on without pushing.

1. Who is the primary buyer for the offer we should prioritize? What problem or situation leads them to look for it, what usually triggers the purchase, and what are they using or doing instead?

2. Which products or offers are you actively advertising right now, what do they cost, what action do you want people to take, and where does the ad send them? Share the destination URL if available, what is on that page, and what the offer is once they land.

3. What actually makes your product work, and why is that different from the alternatives? I am asking about the mechanism—how it delivers the result—not just the benefit.

4. What are the main reasons people do not buy, and what actually answers each one?

5. What proof is approved for use in paid ads? Share exact numbers, customer counts, results, recognizable customers, press, awards, or testimonials, along with any source, date, qualification, or usage restriction. If approval is uncertain, say so.

6. On messaging: what angles or phrases have worked well in advertising, what has fallen flat, and are there any words, claims, or tones you never want used? Where possible, include the platform, campaign objective, approximate date, and metric behind “worked” or “fell flat.” Informal feedback is useful too; identify it as such.

7. What ad formats have worked historically, and what can you actually produce right now? For example: founder on camera, UGC, product footage, static design, or a library of existing ads. Where available, include the platform, approximate date, and results supporting your answer.

8. Who is this product NOT for, and are there any legal or compliance limits on what you can claim? Skip this if neither applies.

9. Are there specific competitors you want me to focus on when we do competitor ad research later? Share their websites and any differences you know matter to your buyers, if available.

PHASE 3: BUILD THE FILE

After I have answered or skipped all questions, write the finished document using exactly the structure below.

Writing and evidence rules:
- Use my language wherever I gave you specific phrasing.
- Use confirmed answers and sourced research. Do not invent facts, proof, results, claims, competitive differences, or lessons from performance.
- Label unresolved inferences “Unconfirmed.”
- Write “Not provided” only when neither my answers nor your research supplies the information. Skipping a question does not invalidate sourced research.
- If my answers conflict with the website, flag the discrepancy. Use information I explicitly confirm as current for operational details such as prices and offers. If the conflict remains unresolved, label it.
- Distinguish measured performance from anecdotal feedback. Do not describe an angle or format as a proven performer without supporting evidence.
- For proof items, distinguish the source of the claim from permission to use it. Record paid-ad approval as “Approved by user,” “Not approved,” or “Approval unknown.” Do not treat user approval as independent verification or legal clearance.
- Quote testimonials verbatim. If exact wording is unavailable, say so rather than reconstructing a quote.
- Preserve qualifications that affect the meaning of a claim, such as timeframe, sample size, audience, or conditions.
- Include compact source references beside researched facts, especially facts likely to change.
- Do not invent legal requirements or imply that claims have received legal review.
- Keep the file concise and useful. Avoid repetition, generic marketing language, and filler.

[Company Name] — Brand and Company Context
Last updated: [date]

1. What we do
One paragraph explaining what the company is, what it sells, and to whom.

2. Category and positioning
What market the company sits in and how the brand is positioned within it. Distinguish stated positioning from unconfirmed interpretation.

3. Primary audience and buying context
The primary buyer, the problem or situation prompting interest, the purchase trigger, and what they currently use or do instead. If audiences vary by offer, make those relationships clear.

4. Products and offers
For each product being advertised: what it is, what it costs, how the offer is structured, and whether it is currently an advertising priority. Include currency, billing terms, and material conditions where known.

5. The mechanism
How the product delivers its result and any supported differences from the alternatives. Separate explanations of how it works from evidence that it works.

6. The conversion path
Where ads send people, the destination URL if known, what is on that page, the offer they encounter, and what action counts as a conversion. Identify any known mismatch between the intended ad promise and the landing page.

7. Proof we can use
Numbers, customer counts, results, recognizable customers, press, awards, and testimonials.

For each item, include:
- The exact claim or verbatim quote
- Its source and relevant date, where available
- Material qualifications or restrictions
- Paid-ad approval status

Clearly separate approved items from items with unknown approval or prohibited usage.

8. Objections and counters
Each reason people do not buy, paired with the answer or evidence that addresses it. If no supported counter is available, write “Not provided.”

9. Messaging that works
Angles, phrases, and hooks reported to perform. Preserve specific wording. Include platform, objective, metric, and approximate date where provided. Label anecdotal observations.

10. Messaging that does not work
Angles and phrases that have fallen flat, the evidence or feedback behind that assessment, and any supported lessons. Do not invent an explanation for poor performance.

11. Never say
Words, phrases, claims, and tonal choices that are off limits. Include user-provided compliance restrictions and required qualifications.

12. Voice
How the brand sounds: sentence rhythm, formality, humor, vocabulary, and distinctive patterns.

Include two or three short examples. Prefer actual brand excerpts and identify their source. If you write new examples, label them “Illustrative—not existing or tested brand copy.” If there is insufficient evidence to establish the voice, say so.

13. Who this is not for
Explicit audience exclusions and poor-fit use cases.

14. Formats and production capability
Formats reported to work, supporting results where available, and what the team can realistically produce now. Distinguish historical performance from current production capability.

15. Competitors to watch
Named competitors, their websites where known, and confirmed differences relevant to buyers. If differences have not been established, write “Not established.” Do not conduct a separate competitor research project during this phase.

After the document, add a short maintenance note covering:
- Which sections are thin or contain unresolved assumptions or conflicts
- What single piece of information would most improve the file
- Which time-sensitive facts should be rechecked before future advertising work

Keep the document tight. Every line should help make future advertising more accurate, specific, or effective.
